A. In Russia the school year begins on 1 September, known as the Day of Knowledge. On this morning schools hold a ceremony called the First Bell. Younger children arrive with huge bouquets for their teachers, and one of the oldest students often carries a first-grader on his shoulders while she rings a little bell.

B. Russian schools usually mark students’ work on a scale from one to five. A “five” means excellent, a “four” is good and a “three” is satisfactory. A “two” means that the work has failed, and a “one” is so rare that many students have never seen one. Teachers sometimes add a plus or a minus to make the marks more precise.

C. At the end of school most students take the Unified State Exam. Its results are used both as school-leaving marks and as entrance exams, so a student can apply to several universities at once with the same scores. That is why so many teenagers spend their last school year preparing for it.

D. When you visit a Russian family, you will almost certainly be asked to take your shoes off in the hall. Many hosts keep a pair of slippers for guests. Walking into a flat in street shoes, especially in winter, is considered impolite, because it brings in dirt and snow.

E. If you buy flowers for a Russian friend, make sure there is an odd number of them — three, five or seven. Even numbers of flowers are taken to funerals and memorials. Many florists will politely remind foreigners of this rule before they wrap a bouquet.

F. Russians have three names: a first name, a surname and a patronymic, which is formed from the father’s first name. Ivan, the son of Pyotr, is Ivan Petrovich. In formal situations, for example when talking to teachers, people use the first name and the patronymic together, without “Mr” or “Mrs”.

G. Millions of Russian city families have a dacha, a small country house with a garden. From May to September they spend their weekends there, growing vegetables, making jam and relaxing in a steam bath. For many people the dacha is not just a hobby but a way of life.

My year in Tomsk

When I told my friends in Manchester that I was going to spend a year studying Russian in Tomsk, most of them had to look it up on a map. Tomsk is a Siberian city of about half a million people, and it is one of Russia’s oldest university towns: Tomsk State University, founded in 1878, was the first university in Siberia. I chose it for a simple reason. My grammar was good, but I could hardly hold a conversation, and I wanted to live somewhere where nobody would switch to English to make my life easier.

My first weeks were harder than I expected. The language was not the main problem; the rules of everyday life were. I smiled at people on the bus, as I would at home, and they looked at me as if something was wrong. My Russian roommate, Dasha, later explained that in Russia a smile is usually kept for people you know, and that smiling at strangers can look insincere or even silly. “It doesn’t mean we’re unfriendly,” she said. “We just save our smiles for real reasons.”

Dasha turned out to be my best teacher. She corrected my mistakes without ever making me feel stupid, and she taught me things that no textbook mentions: never to whistle indoors, never to shake hands across a doorway and always to bring something to the table when you are invited for dinner. When I visited her family in a village outside the city, her grandmother kept filling my plate, and I learnt that saying “I’m full” once is not enough. You have to say it at least three times.

The academic side was different too. Most exams at the university were oral: you pick a card with two questions from a pile, get some time to prepare and then talk to the examiner face to face. At first this terrified me. Later I realised that it taught me to think in Russian rather than translate in my head, which was exactly what I had come for.

And then there was the winter. In January the temperature fell to minus thirty-five, and for a week classes for younger schoolchildren were cancelled. University students, however, were expected to come as usual. I bought felt boots called valenki, which looked ridiculous on me but kept my feet warm, and I discovered that Siberians do not hide at home in the cold. They go skating, skiing and even swimming in ice holes.

Looking back, the most valuable thing I brought home was not my Russian, although my teachers say it is now fluent. It was the habit of looking at familiar things from another side. I still don’t smile at strangers on the bus in Manchester, and my friends think it’s a joke. It isn’t. It’s a small piece of Siberia that I decided to keep.

Задания 3–9

Host: Today my guest is Ivan Petrov, who teaches English at a small village school in the Altai Mountains. Ivan, how did you end up there?

Ivan: Well, after university in Novosibirsk I planned to work as a translator. But in my final year I volunteered at a summer camp in Altai, and I fell in love with the place, and with teaching. So when the village school advertised a job, I applied, partly out of curiosity.

Host: How big is the school?

Ivan: We have just forty-two pupils from the first to the eleventh grade, so some classes have only three or four students. It sounds strange, but it’s actually an advantage: I know every child’s strengths and problems, and nobody can hide at the back of the room.

Host: What’s the biggest challenge?

Ivan: Honestly, it’s not the weather or the distance, although the road to the district centre is sometimes closed after heavy snow. It’s the internet. When it’s slow, we can’t use online lessons or videos, so I’ve learnt to plan everything twice: with and without technology.

Host: Do your students really need English in a village?

Ivan: More than you’d think. Tourists come here from all over the world in summer, and several families run guest houses. My older students help their parents talk to the guests, and that gives them a real reason to learn.

Host: Have any of your students taken the state exam in English?

Ivan: Last year two girls took it, and both scored over eighty points. One of them is now studying tourism management in Barnaul. I was prouder than when I passed my own exams!

Host: Would you ever move back to a big city?

Ivan: People ask me that all the time, and my parents would like me to. But I think I’m more useful here than I would be in Novosibirsk, where there are hundreds of English teachers. Maybe one day, but not in the next few years.

Host: And finally, what advice would you give young teachers?

Ivan: Don’t be afraid of small places. You get more freedom to try new ideas, and you see the results of your work every single day.
