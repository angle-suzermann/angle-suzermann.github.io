Reading · Part 1 — America's National Parks

Last year, about 6.5 million people visited the United States from overseas. Most visitors entered the country through one of the largest cities – New York, Boston, Miami, Los Angeles, San Francisco. Then, having landed in a city, the newcomers simply "stayed put," not realising that the interior of the country offered travel opportunities of unusual beauty and interest. Did you know that between Maine and California there are 41 national parks? Some parks cover thousands of acres. Each has its own distinctive scenery and character. If you like nature study, hiking, camping, bird-watching, clean air, quiet, or just getting away from the city, you will love the national parks.

On the East Coast, for example, there is Acadia National Park, Maine. It faces the Atlantic Ocean and has a long, rocky coastline. On the West Coast, in California, there are Yosemite, with majestic mountains, forests and rivers, and Death Valley, with desert plants and animals. In between are enough national and state parks to satisfy every taste and interest: Cape Hatteras, North Carolina; Great Smoky Mountains, Tennessee; Shenandoah, Virginia. Above all, there is the Grand Canyon, Arizona, that mighty chasm carved in the earth by the Colorado River. Visiting a national park need not be expensive for the overseas visitor, but it does require advance information and careful planning.

Visiting the Grand Canyon

The Grand Canyon is an impressive sight. It is 277 miles long, between 4 and 18 miles wide, and more than a mile deep. No one knows for sure how the canyon was made, but geologists generally agree that over millions of years, the Colorado River, which runs down the centre of the canyon, gradually cut through the sand and rock to carve this mighty chasm. This wearing-down process is called erosion. The wind and natural land movements did the rest. Today the Grand Canyon is a geologist's dream. The rock layers in the sides of the canyon are exposed to view. Each layer is a record of all the natural events that happened to the land through the eons of time. In one layer, there is dust from an ancient volcano. In another layer, there is sand from a lake that once covered the area. In a third layer, there are the bones of prehistoric animals. The canyon is like a storybook: if you had enough knowledge, you could unravel the history of the earth.

But studying geology is not the only interesting thing to do there. It is an ideal place to hike, ride a bike, take a boat ride down the river, camp out overnight, take photographs and see beautiful scenery. To add further interest, the canyon has six different climate zones, and many kinds of plants, trees and animals.

Reading · Part 2 — José and Ibrahim's trip

"We've got to see the Grand Canyon," said José to his friend Ibrahim. José is from Colombia, South America, and speaks Spanish. Ibrahim is from Egypt, and speaks Arabic. To communicate, they have to speak English.

"Let's go," said Ibrahim. "School doesn't start for three weeks. We'll just have time. I saved some money from my summer job, but how will we get there? We don't have a car."

"There are three ways," José said. "We could fly or go by train, but that would be expensive, so let's take the bus. It explains how in this booklet. There are two major interurban bus lines in the U.S. – Greyhound and Trailways," said José, reading from the booklet. "These two companies operate 6,700 buses and carry over 350,000,000 passengers each year – more than the railroads and the airlines combined. Best of all, bus travel is relatively cheap."

"This sounds like a great trip, and practical, too. When do we start?" asked Ibrahim.

"What about Monday? That would give us plenty of time to pack."

"Monday is okay with me," Ibrahim said. "I'm taking one suitcase, some casual clothes, and my hiking boots."

"'Travel light.' That's my motto," José said. "And carry traveler's checks. They're safer than cash."

Monday rolled around in no time. The two friends caught the bus at the depot. The next morning, they were in Flagstaff, Arizona, gateway city to the Grand Canyon. A local bus took them the rest of the way, about 80 miles, and dropped them at the park entrance.

Now their excitement began to rise. They could hardly wait to view the canyon itself. But they were in for a surprise. At first there was nothing special to see. Dark green pine trees surrounded the boys on every side. They seemed to be in the middle of a forest. Fortunately, the trail to the canyon was well marked. "Grand Canyon this way," read a sign.

It took ten minutes of rapid walking to reach the canyon. The boys stumbled on it, really, for there was no preparation, no gradual slope – just flat, wooded country that suddenly disappeared into the mile-deep abyss.

"Wow," said José. "Look at that!"

"Wow," repeated Ibrahim, unable to find better words.

It was a thrilling sight. The rocky precipice at the top fell straight down to the canyon floor, which was very uneven and covered with rocks. Cutting down the middle was the wild Colorado River, although from this distance it looked like a harmless stream. The other side of the canyon was clearly visible. Overhead, a hawk flew lazily in a cloudless sky. The boys could see for a hundred miles in every direction.

"What will we do first?" José asked.

Just at that moment, the boys noticed a park ranger standing beside a big picture of the canyon. "Right now, we're standing on the south rim, or edge, of the canyon, just about here," the ranger said, indicating a place on the picture. "That's the north rim over there," he said, pointing across the canyon. "Did you notice how the rocks and earth are arranged like layers in a cake, and that each layer has a different colour? The layer at the bottom of the canyon is the oldest. It's called Early Precambrian," the ranger said, giving the scientific name. "These rocks must be about 1.7 billion years old. The rocks at the top are younger. Each layer has a different age, and has characteristic deposits that correspond to the geological and natural events occurring at the time. We find evidence of animal life at this level," said the ranger, pointing to a layer near the top. "Probably dinosaurs, extinct animals that lived millions of years ago."

"Isn't this interesting," said Ibrahim. "When I get back to school, I'm going to do some research in geology and write a paper for my science class."

"Yes, it is interesting," said José, "but I'm getting hungry. Isn't it time for supper?"

There were several places to eat. The boys could get fast food at a lunch stand or dinner in the restaurant.

"Let's go to the restaurant," said Ibrahim. "I'm starved."

After a short wait, they were seated at a table. The waiter arrived to take their order.

"I am so hungry, I could eat a dinosaur," said José, glancing at the menu. "I'm going to have vegetable soup, steak, mashed potatoes, lettuce salad, and ice cream for dessert."

Ibrahim ordered mushroom soup, veal stew with rice, tomato salad, and apple pie.

When the boys had finished eating, they glanced out of the window. The sun was just setting. If they hurried, they could just get outside in time to watch the sun drop below the canyon rim, which it did with a burst of many colors.

"Wasn't this a great day?" José said. "What'll we do tomorrow?"
